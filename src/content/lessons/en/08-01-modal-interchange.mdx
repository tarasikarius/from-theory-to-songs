This module is the "why does that chord sound so good?" module. It is the difference between correct and **interesting**.

## The idea

A song in C major can **borrow** chords from C minor, or from any other mode built on C. The tonic stays the same and the color changes for a moment. This is called modal interchange.

The most useful borrowed chords in a major key:

| Borrowed chord | In C | Sound and classic use |
|---|---|---|
| iv | Fm | A bittersweet gut-punch. IV → iv → I is the emotional pivot of "Creep" and a favorite Beatles move. |
| ♭VII | B♭ | Rock swagger. I – ♭VII – IV is the "Sweet Home Alabama" move. |
| ♭VI | A♭ | An epic lift. ♭VI – ♭VII – I is the "heroic ending" cadence of metal, film and game music. |
| ♭III | E♭ | Hard-rock crunch: I – ♭III – IV. |
| iiø7 | Dm7♭5 | Sophisticated melancholy before V. |
| v (minor) | Gm | A softened, dreamy dominant. |

And the other direction, a minor-key song borrowing from major:

| Borrowed chord | In A minor | Sound |
|---|---|---|
| IV (major) | D | Dorian brightness: i – IV |
| I (major) at the very end | A | The **Picardy third**: a minor song that ends on a major chord. Sudden light. |
| ♭II | B♭ | Phrygian darkness, a half step above the tonic. A metal staple. |

**The mechanism.** Borrowed chords bring in notes from outside the key: A♭, B♭ and E♭ in C major. Those chromatic notes pull on ears that are used to the diatonic ones. Use them at emotional pivot points, not everywhere.

## On your instrument

**Guitar: borrowing is one fret away.** In the key of G:

| Move | Chords | What your hand does |
|---|---|---|
| IV → iv | C → Cm | Lower the 3rd of the chord by one fret |
| I → ♭VII | G → F | Move the barre shape down two frets |
| ♭VI → ♭VII → I | E♭ → F → G | One shape, moving up in whole steps |
| I → ♭III | G → B♭ | Move the barre shape up three frets |

With power chords, ♭III, ♭VI and ♭VII are the same shapes you used for the minor key in lesson 4.4. In rock, mixing major-key and minor-key power chords over one root is so common that nobody calls it borrowing. It is just the style.

**Piano.** Play F major, then lower the A to A♭. Then resolve to C major. One finger moved one key, and the mood changed completely.

## Worked example

"Creep" loops four chords for the whole song: G – B – C – Cm. In G major that is I – III – IV – iv.

Follow one inner voice through the loop:

| Chord | G | B | C | Cm |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Inner voice | D | D♯ | E | E♭ |

The line climbs by half steps, D, D♯, E, and then falls back through E♭ to D as the loop restarts. The B major chord and the C minor chord are both outside the key, and both exist to serve that chromatic line. The moment the E drops to E♭ is the borrowed iv, and it is where the song aches.

## Exercises

1. Play I – IV – iv – I in three keys. Over the change from IV to iv, play or sing the melody notes 6, ♭6, 5 (in C: A, A♭, G). Feel the ache.
2. Write an 8-bar chorus in a major key using exactly **one** borrowed chord, at the most emotional word or moment.
3. Metal: write a riff progression i – ♭VI – ♭VII – i. On its final repeat, end ♭VI – ♭VII – **I major** (the Picardy third). Instant album-closer energy.
4. Take a plain I – V – vi – IV song you know and replace one chord with a borrowed one. Try iv instead of IV, or ♭VII instead of V.

## Common mistakes

- **Borrowing in every bar.** If every chord is special, none is. One borrowed chord per section is usually enough.
- **Ignoring the melody.** A melody that sings A while the band plays Fm (with A♭) will clash. When you borrow a chord, check the melody note above it.
