## The idea

There is no single right order for writing a song. There are reliable **pipelines**, and it pays to rotate between them so you do not always get the same kind of song.

| Pipeline | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Riff-up** (rock and metal) | Riff → drum feel → section riffs with 3 + 1 variations → vocal or lead melody on top → structure → lyrics |
| **Chords and hum** (pop) | Four-chord loop → hum melodies and record everything → the best hum becomes the chorus → build the verse as contrast → lyrics from the vowel sounds of the hum |
| **Title first** | A phrase you overheard → say it aloud until it has a rhythm → that rhythm is the chorus hook → harmony to match its mood |
| **Melody first** | A melody hummed into your phone → find the chords it implies (which chord tones does it outline?) → structure |

**The finishing rules.** This is where most songwriters fail.

1. **A finished bad song teaches more than ten brilliant fragments.** Finish ugly, edit later.
2. **Separate the roles.** The writer generates and does not judge. The editor judges and does not generate. Give them different sessions.
3. **Time-box.** One song per week for the next eight weeks, whatever its quality. Demo each one.
4. **The 24-hour test.** Listen the next day. Fix only the moment where your attention drops first. Repeat.

**A demo is a sketch.** Its job is to capture the song so you can judge it and show it. The minimum is one instrument and a voice or lead line recorded on a phone. A step up is a free DAW with a click track, a programmed or looped drum beat, a rhythm guitar, a bass line and the melody. Do not polish the sound. Polish the song.

## On your instrument

**A one-session song sketch, in 60 minutes:**

| Minutes | Do |
|---|---|
| 0–10 | Generate: three riffs or three chord loops. Record each. No judging. |
| 10–15 | Pick one. Decide key, tempo and feel. Start a drum loop or the beat grid. |
| 15–30 | Write the contrasting section: if you have a verse, write the chorus. |
| 30–45 | Hum or play a melody over both. Keep the best take of each. |
| 45–55 | Decide the form on paper: sections, bar counts, energy map. |
| 55–60 | Record one continuous run-through from start to end. |

## Worked example

The riff-up pipeline, applied to the riff from lesson 5.3.

| Step | Decision |
|---|---|
| Riff | The E Phrygian "give me the money now" riff, 100 BPM |
| Drum feel | Basic rock beat for the verse, half-time for the breakdown |
| Sections | Verse: the riff, palm-muted. Chorus: E5 – C5 – D5 – E5 (i – ♭VI – ♭VII – i), open chords. |
| Melody | Chorus melody targets G over E5, then E over C5, F♯ over D5, and E to finish: the 3rd of each chord, then home |
| Structure | Intro (riff alone) – Verse – Chorus – Verse – Chorus – Breakdown (riff in half-time) – Solo over the chorus chords – Chorus twice |
| Ending | Final chorus ends ♭VI – ♭VII – I major: the Picardy third from lesson 8.1 |

Every decision in that table comes from an earlier lesson. That is what the course has been building toward: you no longer wait for ideas. You have a method for producing them and a method for finishing them.

## Capstone assignments

**Song 2 (pop or rock, with lyrics).** Use the chords-and-hum pipeline. It must contain one borrowed chord, a pre-chorus, and a bridge that starts on a chord other than the tonic.

**Song 3 (instrumental rock or metal).** Use the riff-up pipeline. It must contain a modal riff (Phrygian or Dorian), a half-time breakdown, a lead harmonized in 3rds, and a Picardy or ♭VI – ♭VII – I ending.

**Song 4 (free).** Any pipeline, but write the energy map and the arrangement chart **first**, and obey them.

## Common mistakes

- **Polishing the first eight bars forever.** Move on. The rest of the song will tell you what the first eight bars need.
- **Judging while writing.** If you delete ideas as you have them, you will run out of ideas.
- **Waiting for inspiration.** The schedule is the inspiration. One song a week.
