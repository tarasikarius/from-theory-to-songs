## The idea

**The section vocabulary:**

| Section | Job |
|---|---|
| Intro | Sets the mood and often previews the main riff or hook |
| Verse | Tells the story. Lower energy. The lyrics change each time. |
| Pre-chorus | A ramp. Builds tension toward the chorus. |
| Chorus | The payoff: title, hook, biggest energy. The lyrics repeat. |
| Post-chorus | An echo of the hook, often instrumental |
| Bridge (middle 8) | Contrast. Appears once and goes somewhere new. |
| Solo | An instrumental feature, usually over the verse or chorus chords |
| Breakdown | Metal and hardcore: a half-time, stripped-down riff section |
| Outro | The exit: fade, big ending or sudden stop |

**Common blueprints:**

| Type | Structure |
|---|---|
| Pop and rock standard | Intro, Verse 1, Pre-chorus, Chorus, Verse 2, Pre-chorus, Chorus, Bridge, Chorus, Chorus |
| Classic AABA (32 bars) | Verse, Verse, Bridge, Verse: "Yesterday" |
| Riff rock | Riff intro, Verse, Chorus, Verse, Chorus, Solo over the verse riff, Chorus, Chorus |
| 12-bar blues | The progression is the form. Lyrics follow an AAB pattern in each 12 bars. |
| Metal journey | Intro, Riff A, Riff B (verse), Chorus, Riff A varied, Chorus, Breakdown, Solo, final Chorus or Riff A |

In riff rock, sections are often defined by **arrangement changes over the same riff** as much as by new chords. Progressive metal chains riffs into longer suites, but the principle of contrast and return still applies: the listener needs to recognize something coming back.

**Section lengths** are almost always multiples of four bars: 8 or 16 for a verse, 8 for a chorus, 4 or 8 for a pre-chorus. Breaking that expectation with a 6-bar or 9-bar section is a strong effect. Use it deliberately.

**The energy map.** Before writing, sketch the song as a line of intensity over time. The typical shape starts at a medium or low level, peaks in each chorus, dips or detours in the bridge, and reaches its summit in the final chorus, which adds a layer, a key lift or stacked vocals.

## On your instrument

- **Play the form alone.** Take a song you know and play it from start to finish on one guitar, announcing each section out loud as it starts. If you get lost, you do not know the form yet.
- **Mark the boundaries with your playing.** With a single guitar you can still make sections: palm-muted single notes for the verse, open chords for the chorus, something sparse and high for the bridge.
- **Count bars, not seconds.** Write the length of each section in bars. You will see how regular most songs are.

## Worked example

"Smells Like Teen Spirit" uses the same four power chords for nearly the whole song. The form is created almost entirely by dynamics.

| Section | What happens | Energy |
|---|---|---|
| Intro | The riff, first on a clean guitar alone, then distorted with the full band | High |
| Verse | The guitar drops to a sparse two-note clean figure. Bass and drums carry it. Quiet vocal. | Low |
| Pre-chorus | The guitar part gets insistent and the vocal repeats one word | Rising |
| Chorus | Full distortion, shouted vocal, crashing cymbals | Peak |
| Post-chorus | A short instrumental tag with a new riff | High |
| Verse 2, Pre-chorus, Chorus | The same cycle again | |
| Solo | The guitar plays the verse vocal melody | Medium |
| Verse 3, Pre-chorus, final Chorus | The last chorus is extended and more intense | Summit |

Two lessons here. First, a song does not need many chord progressions: it needs **contrast between sections**. Second, the solo quotes the vocal melody instead of showing off, which keeps the song focused on one idea.

## Exercises

1. Map three favorite songs: a timeline of sections with bar counts, an energy graph, and a note of **what changes** at each boundary. Is it the instrumentation, the register, the density or the key?
2. Take your best riff, your best progression and your best melody from the earlier modules. Design a full structure on paper, with sections, bar counts and an energy graph, **before** writing the missing parts. Then write them. **This is Assignment Song 1: a complete instrumental demo.**
3. Take one of your mapped songs and rearrange its sections on paper: start with the chorus, or remove the bridge. Describe what the song would lose.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing sections with no plan for the whole.** Great parts that do not contrast with each other add up to a flat song.
- **Too many sections.** Most songs need three or four distinct ideas. If you have seven, you have two songs.
- **Skipping the energy map.** If the first chorus is already at maximum, the last one has nowhere to go.
