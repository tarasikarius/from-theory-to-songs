## The idea

Modulation means the home note moves. There are four practical techniques.

**1. Pivot chord.** Use a chord that belongs to both keys, and reinterpret it. Am is vi in C major and ii in G major. Enter it as one, leave it as the other, and confirm the new key with its V7: C – Am – D7 – G.

**2. Direct modulation.** Start the new section in the new key with no preparation. Pop does this constantly between a verse in A minor and a chorus in C major. Relative keys share all their notes, so the change barely feels like a modulation.

**3. The step-up lift.** The final chorus jumps up a half step or a whole step, usually through one bar of the new key's V7. It is known as the "truck driver's gear change". It is cheesy when lazy and glorious when earned. "Livin' on a Prayer" jumps up a minor 3rd for its last choruses.

**4. Common tone and chromatic slide.** Hold one note and shift the chord under it. This is a film-score move. Metal does something related all the time: moving the same riff up or down a minor 3rd or a half step is, in effect, a modulation.

**How far is the new key?** Distance on the circle of fifths tells you how dramatic a modulation will feel:

| Move | Example | Feels |
|---|---|---|
| Relative | A minor ↔ C major | Like a change of light, not of place |
| Neighbor on the circle | C → G, C → F | Smooth: only one note changes |
| Parallel | C major ↔ C minor | Same place, different weather |
| Step up | G → A, C → D♭ | A lift: everything gets higher and brighter |
| Far across the circle | C → F♯, C → A♭ | A jump cut |

Relative, parallel and step-up moves cover about 90% of songwriting needs.

## On your instrument

- **Guitar: the step-up is the easy one.** With barre chords and power chords, moving a whole section up two frets is trivial. That is the reason it is overused. Make sure the song has earned it: the last chorus should already be at full arrangement before the key goes up.
- **Check the singer.** A lift of a whole step puts the top note of the chorus a whole step higher. Find out whether the singer has that note before you write it into the song.
- **Open strings do not modulate.** A riff built on the open low E cannot move up a step without changing its character. Either retune the idea to fretted notes, or modulate to a key that keeps the open string useful: E to A, or E minor to G major.

## Worked example

A final-chorus lift from G major to A major. The chorus ends on G. The new chorus starts on A. You need one connecting bar.

| Option | Connecting bar | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| The new dominant | E7 | E7 is V7 of A. The classic solution. |
| IV – V of the new key | D → E | D is shared: it is V of the old key and IV of the new one. A pivot chord. |
| No preparation | A drum fill | Direct modulation. The surprise is the effect. |

Play G – E7 – A and then G – D – E – A. In the second version, the D sounds like home territory and only the E tells you something has changed. That is a pivot chord at work: the listener crosses the border without noticing where it was.

## Exercises

1. Write a verse in A minor and a chorus in C major (relative keys). Then rewrite the chorus in A **major** (parallel). Compare the two lifts.
2. Engineer a final-chorus modulation from G to A. Try all three connecting bars from the worked example and keep the one that suits your song.
3. Take a riff of yours and play it a minor 3rd higher as a new section. Then find a one-bar transition that makes the jump sound intended.
4. Find a song in your listening that changes key. Work out the two keys and identify which of the four techniques it uses.

## Common mistakes

- **Modulating because the song is boring.** A key change does not fix a weak chorus. It only makes it higher.
- **Forgetting the way back.** If a bridge modulates, plan how it returns. The V7 of the home key is the usual door.
