This is where theory becomes songwriting. After this module you should never again wonder "what chord comes next?". You will have a map.

## The idea

Take a major scale. On each note, stack two more scale notes a 3rd apart. Use only notes of the scale. You get seven **diatonic chords**: the chord family of the key.

| Degree | I | ii | iii | IV | V | vi | vii° |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| In C major | C | Dm | Em | F | G | Am | Bdim |
| Quality | major | minor | minor | major | major | minor | diminished |
| With a 7th | Cmaj7 | Dm7 | Em7 | Fmaj7 | G7 | Am7 | Bm7♭5 |

The pattern is the same in every major key: **major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished.**

Change the key and watch the chords change while the numerals stay:

<progression-player tonic="C" numerals="I ii iii IV V vi vii°" tempo="80"></progression-player>

**Roman numerals** are the transposable language of songwriting. Upper case means major, lower case means minor, and ° means diminished. "I – V – vi – IV" describes thousands of songs at once. "C – G – Am – F" describes them in one key only. Think in numerals, and translate to your key or capo position last.

Notice that V is the only chord that becomes a dominant 7th when you add a 7th. That is why G7 points so clearly to C: no other chord in the key has that sound.

## On your instrument

The keys guitarists use most. Play each row as open or barre chords, saying the numeral as you play.

| Key | I | ii | iii | IV | V | vi | vii° |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C | C | Dm | Em | F | G | Am | Bdim |
| G | G | Am | Bm | C | D | Em | F♯dim |
| D | D | Em | F♯m | G | A | Bm | C♯dim |
| A | A | Bm | C♯m | D | E | F♯m | G♯dim |
| E | E | F♯m | G♯m | A | B | C♯m | D♯dim |
| F | F | Gm | Am | B♭ | C | Dm | Edim |

**A neck shortcut.** With the I chord as a barre chord rooted on string 6, the IV is at the same fret rooted on string 5, and the V is two frets higher on string 5. The vi is three frets below the I on string 6. This map is the same at every fret, so you can play I, IV, V and vi in any key without thinking about note names.

**Piano.** Play the seven triads of C major up the keyboard using only white keys, keeping the same hand shape. You will hear the major, minor and diminished qualities appear without doing anything. The scale decides them.

## Worked example

Converting a song to numerals. The verse of "Let It Be" is C – G – Am – F.

1. The key is C major, so C is I.
2. G is the 5th note of the scale and the chord is major: V.
3. Am is built on the 6th note and is minor: vi.
4. F is built on the 4th note and is major: IV.

The verse is **I – V – vi – IV**. To play it in G, read the G row of the table: G – D – Em – C. To play it in A: A – E – F♯m – D. You have just transposed a song without moving a single note by hand.

## Exercises

1. Write the diatonic triads with numerals for G, D, A, E and F major from memory. Check against the table.
2. Take five songs you know and convert their chords to Roman numerals. You will immediately see the same skeletons repeating.
3. Play I – IV – V – I in five keys using the neck shortcut, without looking up any chord names.
4. Add 7ths: play Imaj7 – vi7 – ii7 – V7 in C and in G with the shell voicings from lesson 3.2.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing "iii" for a major chord.** Case carries the quality. III and iii are different chords.
- **Naming numerals from the first chord of the song.** Numerals are measured from the tonic of the key, wherever the song starts.
