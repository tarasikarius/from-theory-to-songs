## The idea

Intervals are not neutral distances. Each one has a character, and four families cover them all:

| Family | Intervals | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Open consonances | P5, P8 (and P4) | Stable, hollow, powerful |
| Soft consonances | 3rds and 6ths | Warm, sweet, emotional |
| Mild dissonances | M2, m7 | Tension that colors without hurting |
| Sharp dissonances | m2, M7, tritone | Unstable, demanding resolution |

- **Open consonances** are why the power chord works. It has no 3rd, so it does not commit to major or minor. Under distortion, the simple frequency ratio of the 5th (3:2) stays clean while 3rds turn to mud.
- **Soft consonances** are the sound of twin-guitar harmonies. Iron Maiden and Thin Lizzy lines are mostly parallel 3rds with the occasional 6th.
- **Mild dissonances** are the sound of sus chords and 7th chords: tension you can live in.
- **Sharp dissonances** are instability. The tritone is metal's signature interval: the riff of "Black Sabbath" is built on it. The minor 2nd is the horror-film interval.

Dissonance is not bad. It is fuel. Music moves because tension resolves, and a piece made only of consonance is wallpaper.

**Why distortion changes things.** Distortion adds overtones to every note and also creates new tones from the sum and difference of the notes you play together. With a 5th or an octave those extra tones line up with the original notes, so the sound gets thicker. With a 3rd, and even more with a 2nd or a tritone, the extra tones land between the notes and the result is rough. That is physics, and it explains most of rock rhythm-guitar writing.

## On your instrument

Play these pairs on guitar, first clean, then distorted, and listen to what the distortion does:

| Pair | Where | What you should hear |
|---|---|---|
| Power chord (root and 5th) | 6:5 and 5:7 | Stays clear and gets bigger |
| Major 3rd | 6:5 and 5:4 | Clearly rougher under gain |
| Tritone | 6:5 and 5:6 | Unstable, evil, wants to move |
| Minor 2nd | 2:4 (D♯) and the open 1st string (E) | A grinding beat between the notes |

A tight minor 2nd is awkward to finger on guitar, which is why the open-string trick above is the easy way to get one. It is also why guitar voicings usually spread dissonant notes apart. More on that in Module 3.

On piano, play E and F together in the middle of the keyboard, then move the F up an octave. The same two notes become a minor 9th: still tense, but with more air. **Distance softens dissonance.**

## Worked example

Two riffs on the same rhythm, straight 8th notes, palm-muted. Riff A uses only the root, 5th and octave. Riff B swaps two notes for a tritone (B♭) and a minor 2nd (F).

```
Riff A      1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &
D|----------------2-------2-|
A|--------------2-------2---|
E|----------0-0-----0-0-----|
            1 1 5 8 1 1 5 8

Riff B      1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &
D|------------------------2-|
A|--------------1-----------|
E|----------0-0---0-0-1-0---|
            1 1 TT 1 1 ♭2 1 8
```

Riff A is solid and neutral: it could belong to a punk song or a pop-rock verse. Riff B is immediately darker. Two notes changed, and the genre changed with them.

## Exercises

1. Write a 4-bar riff on the low E string using only roots, 5ths and octaves. Then write a second riff that adds one tritone and one minor 2nd as passing tension. Record both and compare. Which one feels like metal, and why?
2. Harmonize a simple tune you know in parallel 3rds, on guitar with a second recorded track or on piano with both hands. Mark the spots where a 3rd sounds wrong and a 4th or 6th sounds better. You are discovering counterpoint by ear.
3. Play every interval from the first lesson as a two-note chord with distortion on. Sort them into three lists: clean, usable, mud. Keep the list. It is your personal guide to writing rhythm parts.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating dissonance as an error.** If your riffs sound polite, they probably contain no sharp dissonance at all.
- **Leaving dissonance hanging by accident.** A tritone or minor 2nd creates an expectation. Resolve it by step, or leave it unresolved on purpose.
- **Playing full chords with high gain** and then lowering the gain to fix the mud. Remove the 3rd instead.
