## The idea

| Scale | Formula | From A | Sound |
|---|---|---|---|
| Minor pentatonic | 1 ♭3 4 5 ♭7 | A C D E G | Rock lead guitar from Led Zeppelin to Nirvana |
| Major pentatonic | 1 2 3 5 6 | A B C♯ E F♯ | Country, pop and classic-rock brightness |
| Blues scale | 1 ♭3 4 ♭5 5 ♭7 | A C D E♭ E G | Minor pentatonic plus the "blue note" |

**Minor pentatonic** is natural minor with its two sensitive notes removed: the 2 and the ♭6, the notes that create half steps. With no half steps left, it is almost impossible to play a wrong note.

**Major pentatonic** does the same thing to the major scale: it removes 4 and 7. The shapes are identical to minor pentatonic with the root moved. A minor pentatonic and C major pentatonic are the same five notes, exactly like relative keys.

**The blues scale** adds the ♭5 to minor pentatonic. The ♭5 is a **passing** note: squeeze it between 4 and 5, bend into it, slide through it. Do not sit on it.

> **The deep skill.** Pentatonic is not a beginner scale. It is a skeleton. Professionals play pentatonic and add to it: the 2, the 6, the ♭6, chromatic passing notes. They decorate a strong frame. Beginners play seven-note scales and get lost. Switch the explorer between minor pentatonic and natural minor to see the two added notes.

## On your instrument

You probably know "the box": A minor pentatonic at the 5th fret. Now go further.

- **Learn all five boxes**, and more importantly the **diagonal paths** that connect them up the neck. These are the sliding shapes every rock player uses to travel from the 5th fret to the 12th in one phrase.
- **Find the roots in every box.** There are two or three in each. Start and end phrases on them until you can find them without looking.
- **Mix major and minor** (the B.B. King and Eric Clapton move). Over a blues in A, alternate A major pentatonic (sweet) and A minor pentatonic (tough). Rule of thumb: major pentatonic over the I chord, minor pentatonic over the IV and V chords.
- **Bends are part of the scale.** The classic targets are 4 bent up to 5, ♭7 bent up to 1, and ♭3 bent slightly sharp toward the major 3rd (the "blues curl").

## Worked example

A riff in E minor pentatonic on the two lowest strings, palm-muted, straight 8th notes. Three bars are identical and the fourth changes its ending:

```
Bars 1, 2, 3     1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &
A|-----------------------0-2-----|
E|---------------0-0-3-0-----3-0-|
                 1 1 ♭3 1 4 5 ♭3 1

Bar 4            1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &
A|-----------------------0-2-5-7-|
E|---------------0-0-3-0---------|
                 1 1 ♭3 1 4 5 ♭7 1
```

The variation climbs to the ♭7 and the octave instead of falling back. Repeating an idea three times and changing the fourth is most of what riff writing is. Module 5 turns this into a system.

## Exercises

1. **Two notes per bar.** Loop the 12-bar blues above and solo using at most two notes in each bar. This forces phrasing instead of noodling.
2. Write a 4-bar riff in E minor pentatonic on the bottom two strings, palm-muted. Then write variation B by changing only the last bar.
3. Work out the first four bars of any pentatonic solo you love, by ear, roughly. Label the degrees.
4. Play the same lick in A minor pentatonic and then three frets lower in A major pentatonic, over an A chord. Notice that the notes that sounded tough now sound sweet.

## Common mistakes

- **Living in one box.** The first box is a room. The neck is a house.
- **Sitting on the blue note.** Held for a whole bar, the ♭5 sounds like a mistake. Passed through, it sounds like the blues.
- **Running the scale up and down.** A scale is an alphabet. Nobody wants to hear the alphabet.
