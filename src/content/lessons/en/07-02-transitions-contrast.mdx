## The idea

Sections are easy. **Seams are craft.**

**Lifts into the chorus:**

- End the pre-chorus on V, a half cadence, so the chorus arrives as the resolution.
- A drum fill, or a bar of silence: stop, then slam.
- A rising melodic line or a rising bass line.
- A "runway" chord: one extra bar of IV or ♭VII before the chorus.
- A dropout: everything stops for one beat before the downbeat of the chorus.

**Contrast levers.** Change at least two of these at each section boundary:

| Lever | Example |
|---|---|
| Harmonic rhythm | Verse: one chord per two bars. Chorus: two chords per bar. |
| Register | Verse low, chorus high |
| Density | Verse: guitar, bass, drums. Chorus: add a second guitar and backing vocals. |
| Subdivision | Verse in 8ths, chorus in 16ths, or the drums move from hi-hat to ride and crashes |
| Chord palette | Verse centered on vi, chorus centered on I |
| Dynamics | Quiet verse, loud chorus |

**The purpose of a bridge** is to go somewhere new. Start on a chord you have not led with, such as IV, vi or a borrowed chord from Module 8, or shift key or mode. Then engineer the return, usually by landing on the V of the home key.

**The boredom test.** Every eight bars, something should change, however small: a fill, a new layer, a melodic variation. Loop-based writing survives on this drip of novelty.

## On your instrument

Practice transitions the way you practice licks: isolate them. Loop only the last two bars of the verse and the first two bars of the chorus, and try each of these in turn:

- **Stop and slam.** Mute everything on beat 4, then hit the chorus.
- **Rising ramp.** Walk the bass note or the top note up by step into the chorus chord.
- **Rhythmic build.** Play 8ths, then 16ths, for the last bar.
- **The pick-up.** Start the chorus riff or melody one beat before the bar line.

## Worked example

Getting from a verse in E minor to a chorus in G major, three ways. The verse loops Em – C. The chorus starts on G.

| Transition | Last bars of the verse | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Half cadence | Em – C – **D** (one full bar) | D is V of G. The chorus resolves it. Classic and clear. |
| Stop and slam | Em – C, then silence on beat 4 | The gap makes the chorus hit harder than any chord could. |
| Runway | Em – C – **Am – D**, two chords in the last bar | The harmony speeds up as the chorus approaches: ii – V into I. |

All three work. The first is neutral, the second is aggressive, and the third is polished. The choice is part of the song's character.

## Exercises

1. In Assignment Song 1, build two different verse-to-chorus transitions: one "stop and slam" and one "rising ramp". Keep the better one.
2. Write a bridge for it that starts on a chord the song has not emphasized and returns home through V.
3. Apply the boredom test to your song. Go through it eight bars at a time and write down what changes in each block. Fix every block where the answer is "nothing".
4. Find one song where the chorus uses exactly the same chords as the verse. List the levers the writers used to make it feel like a different section.

## Common mistakes

- **Changing only the chords.** New chords with the same rhythm, register and density do not make a new section.
- **Bridges that do not come back.** A bridge needs an exit. Plan the last bar first.
- **Transitions every four bars.** If every seam has a fill, a stop and a riser, none of them means anything.
