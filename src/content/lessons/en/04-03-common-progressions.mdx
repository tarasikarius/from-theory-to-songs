## The idea

Learn these as **sounds**, not as trivia. Each row is one sound you should be able to recognize and play in any key.

| Progression | Sound and famous examples |
|---|---|
| I – V – vi – IV | The pop axis: "Let It Be", "No Woman, No Cry", half the radio |
| vi – IV – I – V | The same loop starting on the minor chord: "Zombie", "Numb" |
| I – IV – V (– IV) | Rock and roll primary colors: "Wild Thing", "La Bamba" |
| I – vi – IV – V | The 1950s doo-wop loop: "Stand By Me" |
| ii – V – I | The engine of jazz. In pop, the smooth landing at the end of a pre-chorus |
| 12-bar blues | Blues, early rock, countless metal boogies |
| I – ♭VII – IV | Mixolydian rock: "Sweet Home Alabama", "Sympathy for the Devil" |
| i – ♭VII – ♭VI (– ♭VII) | The Aeolian descent: "All Along the Watchtower", the outro solo of "Stairway to Heaven" |
| i – iv – V | The minor lament with the harmonic-minor sting |

**The 12-bar blues** is a progression and a form at once:

| Bars 1–4 | Bars 5–8 | Bars 9–12 |
|---|---|---|
| I – I – I – I | IV – IV – I – I | V – IV – I – (V) |

The V in the last bar is the "turnaround": it sends you back to the top.

**The same four chords, heard two ways.** vi – IV – I – V and i – ♭VI – ♭III – ♭VII are the same chords. In "Zombie" the loop is Em – C – G – D. If you hear G as home, it is vi – IV – I – V in G major. If you hear Em as home, and in that song most people do, it is i – ♭VI – ♭III – ♭VII in E minor. Lesson 4.4 looks at minor keys in detail.

**Loop or journey.** Modern pop often **loops** one four-chord cell for the whole song, and the contrast comes from melody and arrangement. Classic songwriting **travels**: the verse progression differs from the chorus progression, and cadences steer the form. Both are valid tools. Choose deliberately.

## On your instrument

- Play every progression in the table in two keys on guitar, and once on piano or with the player.
- **Sing the roots** while you play. This is the fastest way to learn to recognize progressions by ear.
- **Change the harmonic rhythm.** Play I – V – vi – IV with one bar per chord, then with two chords per bar, then with two bars per chord. The same progression becomes a punk song, a ballad and an anthem.

## Worked example

From loop to journey. Start with the loop vi – IV – I – V in G: Em – C – G – D.

An 8-bar journey with the same flavor:

| Bar | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chord | Em | C | G | D | Em | C | Am | D → G |
| Function | T | S | T | D | T | S | S | D → T |

Bars 1 to 4 are the loop. Bars 5 and 6 repeat its start. Bar 7 goes somewhere new: Am, the ii chord, which the loop never used. Bar 8 delivers a real authentic cadence, D to G. The section now has a beginning, a departure and an arrival.

## Exercises

1. **The ten-progression sprint.** In 30 minutes, write ten different four-chord loops as numerals plus one key each. No quality control: volume is the point. Star the two best and keep them for Module 6.
2. Pick one starred loop. Write an 8-bar **journey** version: keep its flavor, add a departure and a real cadence.
3. Play a 12-bar blues in E and in A with power chords, then with the shell voicings from lesson 3.2.
4. Use the trainer below until you can name these progressions by ear.

## Common mistakes

- **Collecting progressions instead of hearing them.** A list of fifty progressions is useless if you cannot recognize five by ear.
- **Believing a progression makes a song.** Thousands of songs share I – V – vi – IV. The melody, rhythm and arrangement make them different.
