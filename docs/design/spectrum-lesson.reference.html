Naming and Building Intervals

An interval is the distance between two notes. Learn to name it, build it, find it on the neck and recognize it by ear.

01The idea 02On your instrument 03Worked example 04Exercises 05Common mistakes 06Check yourself

Everything in music, from scales and chords to melodies and riffs, is built from intervals. Once you know intervals, chords stop being memorized shapes and become transparent structures. This is the highest-leverage module in the course.

01The idea

An interval is the distance between two notes, measured in two ways at once:

  • Number (2nd, 3rd, 4th and so on): count the letter names, including both ends. C to E is C, D, E, so it is a 3rd. Always.
  • Quality (major, minor, perfect, augmented, diminished): the exact number of semitones.

Key facts:

  • Unisons, 4ths, 5ths and octaves are perfect. They do not come in major and minor versions.
  • 2nds, 3rds, 6ths and 7ths come in major and minor versions, one semitone apart.
  • Spelling matters. C–D♯ (augmented 2nd) and C–E♭ (minor 3rd) are the same two frets but mean different things. The letter count decides the number: C to D-something is always some kind of 2nd.
  • Inversions add up to 9. Flip an interval upside down and a 3rd becomes a 6th, a 2nd becomes a 7th, a 4th becomes a 5th. Major becomes minor and perfect stays perfect.

02On your instrument

Guitar: interval shapes are movable. Learn each interval as a small hand shape and it works everywhere on the neck. With the lower note on string 6 or 5, and “next string” meaning the next thinner one:

IntervalShapeExample from 6:5 (A)
m3next string, 2 frets lower5:3 (C)
M3next string, 1 fret lower5:4 (C♯)
P4next string, same fret5:5 (D)
P5next string, 2 frets higher5:7 (E). This is the power chord.
Octavetwo strings over, 2 frets higher4:7 (A)

The B-string rule. Strings are tuned a 4th apart, except strings 3 and 2 (G to B), which are a major 3rd apart. Whenever a shape crosses from string 3 to string 2, move the higher note one fret up. The octave from 4:5 is 2:8, not 2:7.

03Worked example

The “Smoke on the Water” riff, written as intervals above the first note G:

G|------3---5-------3---6-5-------3---5---3-----|
D|--5-------------5-------------5-----------5---|
    1   ♭3  4     1   ♭3  ♭5 4  1   ♭3  4   ♭3  1

only four notes!

Three things to notice. The riff uses only four notes: root, minor 3rd, perfect 4th and the tritone as a passing note. The first move, root to ♭3, is a minor 3rd: next string, 2 frets lower. And the famous “wrong” note is the tritone, which immediately falls a semitone to the 4th.

04Exercises

1

Spelling drill (paper, 10 minutes a day for a week). Write every interval from m2 to M7 above these roots: C, G, D, A, E, B♭, E♭, F♯. Then write them below the same roots.

2

Shape drill (guitar, 5 minutes a day). Pick a random fret on string 6. Play the m3, M3, P4, P5, m7 and octave above it. Say the note names out loud.

3

Ear drill. Use the trainer in lesson 1.3. The goal for two weeks: 90% on ascending intervals, then descending, then harmonic.

4

Real-music hunt. Take three riffs or melodies you already play. Label the interval between every pair of neighboring notes.

05Common mistakes

Counting semitones only. “Three semitones” tells you the size, not the name. Count letters first, then semitones.

Forgetting the B-string rule and wondering why a shape sounds wrong on the top strings.

Learning intervals only upward. Riffs and melodies go down just as often. Practice both directions from day one.
